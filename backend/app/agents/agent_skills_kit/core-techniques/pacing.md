## When to use

Use this skill whenever you need to control the speed and flow of your video to influence viewer emotions, maintain attention, and create dynamic storytelling. Pacing is one of the most powerful tools in an editor's arsenal - it can make a scene feel tense, relaxed, exciting, or contemplative.

## Core Principles

### 1. Understanding Pacing
- **Definition**: Pacing is the speed at which your video unfolds, controlled primarily by shot duration, cut frequency, and rhythm.
- **Why it matters**: Pacing directly affects viewer engagement, emotional response, and information retention. Too fast = overwhelming, too slow = boring.
- **Pacing is emotional**: Fast pacing creates excitement, urgency, chaos. Slow pacing creates tension, contemplation, romance. Varying pace creates dynamic emotional arcs.
- **Genre dictates baseline**: Action films = fast, dramas = slow, thrillers = variable (slow build, fast payoff).

### 2. Shot Duration Control
- **Short shots (0.5-2s)**: Create energy, excitement, chaos. Use for action sequences, montages, hype content.
- **Medium shots (2-5s)**: Standard for dialogue, exposition, most scenes. Allows viewers to absorb information.
- **Long shots (5-15s)**: Create contemplation, tension, awe. Use for establishing shots, emotional moments, scenic beauty.
- **Very long shots (15s+)**: Build intense tension or showcase impressive cinematography. Risk of losing viewer if not compelling.
- **Average shot length (ASL)**: Calculate by dividing total runtime by number of shots. Use as baseline metric.

### 3. Rhythm & Beat
- **Visual rhythm**: The pattern of cuts and shot lengths creates a visual beat. This should complement or contrast with audio rhythm.
- **Consistent rhythm**: Steady, predictable pacing creates comfort (sitcoms, vlogs).
- **Variable rhythm**: Changing pace creates interest and emotional variation. Build from slow to fast for excitement.
- **Accelerando**: Gradually shorten shots to build tension (common in action climaxes).
- **Ritardando**: Gradually lengthen shots to create release or contemplation (common after climax).

### 4. Cutting Frequency
- **High cut frequency**: Many cuts per minute (60+). Creates energy, urgency, modern feel. Used in music videos, action, TikTok.
- **Low cut frequency**: Few cuts per minute (10-20). Creates deliberate, contemplative, cinematic feel. Used in dramas, documentaries.
- **Cutting on action**: Cuts during movement are less noticeable and maintain flow.
- **Cutting on dialogue**: Cuts between speakers during conversation. Use eyeline matches.
- **Cutting on beat**: Align cuts to musical beats for rhythmic satisfaction.

### 5. Time Manipulation
- **Slow motion**: Extends time, emphasizes moments, creates drama. Use 50-80% speed for subtle, 30% for dramatic.
- **Speed ramping**: Smoothly transition between speeds within a clip. Creates dynamic flow.
- **Fast motion (time-lapse)**: Compresses time, shows progression, creates energy.
- **Freeze frame**: Stops time completely for emphasis or comedic effect.
- **Reverse**: Play footage backwards for surreal or comedic moments.

### 6. Pacing by Content Type
- **Action/Thriller**: Fast cuts, short shots, quick dialogue, minimal pauses. ASL: 1-3 seconds.
- **Drama**: Medium to long shots, deliberate cuts, pauses for emotion, ASL: 4-8 seconds.
- **Comedy**: Varied pacing. Quick cuts for jokes, longer takes for awkward tension. ASL: 2-5 seconds.
- **Documentary**: Medium shots, clear pacing for information absorption. ASL: 3-6 seconds.
- **Tutorial/Explainer**: Slow to medium. Allow time for comprehension. ASL: 4-10 seconds.
- **Music video**: Highly variable, often synced to music beat. ASL: 0.5-3 seconds.
- **Commercial/Ad**: Fast hook (first 3 seconds), then varied to maintain interest. ASL: 1-4 seconds.

### 7. Emotional Pacing
- **Tension building**: Start slow, gradually increase cut frequency, shorten shots, add music crescendo.
- **Release**: After climax, lengthen shots, use calm music, show aftermath.
- **Surprise**: Break established rhythm with a sudden very fast or very slow moment.
- **Confusion**: Use disorienting cuts, rapid-fire shots, shaky cam.
- **Romance**: Slow, lingering shots, soft lighting, gentle motion.

### 8. Information Pacing
- **Complex information**: Slow down. Use longer shots, repeat key points, add text reinforcement.
- **Simple information**: Speed up. Quick cuts, minimal exposition.
- **Progressive disclosure**: Reveal information gradually. Don't overwhelm.
- **Redundancy**: Important points should be shown, said, and written for retention.

## Technical Guidelines

### Measuring Pacing
- **Shot length histogram**: Plot distribution of shot durations. Aim for appropriate curve (normal distribution for balanced pacing, skewed for specific effect).
- **Cuts per minute**: Count total cuts divided by minutes. Benchmark: Action (60-100), Drama (10-30), Comedy (20-40).
- **Average shot length (ASL)**: Total duration / number of shots.
- **Motion index**: Amount of camera movement per minute. High motion = faster perceived pace.

### Tools for Pacing Control
- **Timeline zoom**: See cuts clearly. Adjust spacing.
- **Ripple edit**: Trim clips while maintaining timeline continuity.
- **Rolling edit**: Adjust cut point between two clips without changing total duration.
- **Speed/duration**: Change clip speed to adjust shot length without trimming.
- **Time remapping**: Create variable speed within a clip.

### Pacing Markers
- **Chapter markers**: Divide long content into digestible segments.
- **Section titles**: Text overlays indicating new topic.
- **Musical cues**: Changes in music signal pacing shifts.
- **Sound design**: Audio transitions (rises, falls) accompany visual pacing changes.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Monotone pacing**: Same speed throughout = viewer fatigue. Vary it.
- **Too fast for content**: Complex information needs time to land. Don't cut before viewer understands.
- **Too slow for genre**: A thriller with slow pacing loses tension. Match genre expectations.
- **Inconsistent pacing**: Random changes without purpose confuse viewers. Have intent.
- **Ignoring audio**: Pacing should complement music and sound, not fight it.
- **Long static shots**: No movement or change for >5 seconds risks losing attention.
- **Rushed transitions**: Abrupt cuts without reason feel jarring. Use purposeful cuts.
- **No breathing room**: Constant intensity exhausts viewers. Include calm moments.
- **Poor hook pacing**: First 10 seconds must grab. Start with your fastest, most compelling shots.
- **Weak ending**: Final 10 seconds should have appropriate pacing (slow for emotional, fast for energetic). Don't just stop.

## Advanced Techniques

### 1. Psychological Pacing
- **Attention decay**: Viewer attention drops over time. Reset with pattern interrupts (change of scenery, music shift, surprise). Every 60-90 seconds in long-form.
- **Cognitive load**: Complex visuals + complex audio = overload. Simplify one when the other is heavy.
- **Emotional arc**: Map pacing to story structure: exposition (slow), rising action (accelerate), climax (fast), resolution (slow).
- **Subjective time**: Make time feel slower (long takes, slow motion) or faster (fast cuts, time-lapse) based on character experience.

### 2. Cross-Cutting Pacing
- **Parallel editing**: Cut between two simultaneous actions. Speed of cuts creates tension (faster = more urgent).
- **Intercutting**: Insert short shots of secondary action into primary scene. Adds information without breaking main flow.
- **Match cuts**: Cut between visually similar shots. Creates thematic connection and smooth flow.

### 3. Montage Pacing
- **Training montage**: Rapid cuts showing progression. Use consistent beat.
- **Passage of time**: Use time-lapse, changing seasons, aging makeup. Compress long periods.
- **Emotional montage**: Series of meaningful moments that build character or theme. Slower than action montage.

### 4. Dialogue Pacing
- **Pause utilization**: Silence after important lines lets them sink in. Don't be afraid of 1-2 second pauses.
- **Overlapping dialogue**: Characters talking over each other creates realism and urgency.
- **Reaction shots**: Cut to listener's reaction during dialogue. Breaks monotony and shows emotion.
- **J-cut/L-cut**: Audio leads or lags video to smooth dialogue scenes.

### 5. Action Pacing
- **Shot-reverse-shot**: Standard for dialogue/action. Vary duration based on intensity.
- **Continuous action**: Long take following action. Creates immersion and tension (no cuts).
- **Staccato cuts**: Very short shots (0.5s or less) for chaotic, disorienting effect.
- **Slow-motion emphasis**: Highlight key action moments with slow-mo, then return to normal speed.

### 6. Music-Video Sync
- **Cut on every beat**: Every musical beat gets a cut. Creates high-energy, rhythmic feel.
- **Cut on downbeat**: Only cut on the first beat of each measure (stronger rhythm).
- **Phrase-based**: Cut at musical phrase boundaries (4, 8, 16 bars). Creates narrative structure.
- **Contrast**: Sometimes cut against the beat for surprise or disorientation.

## Workflow Recommendations

1. **Assembly**: Place all footage in order without worrying about length.
2. **Rough cut**: Trim to approximate desired duration. Get story right.
3. **Pacing pass**: Watch through and note where it feels too slow or too fast. Mark timestamps.
4. **Shot length analysis**: Check ASL and shot distribution. Adjust outliers.
5. **Cut frequency**: Count cuts per minute in different sections. Ensure variety.
6. **Music integration**: Add music early. Let it guide pacing decisions.
7. **Trim aggressively**: Cut any unnecessary frames. Every second counts.
8. **Test with viewers**: Get feedback on whether pacing feels right. Adjust based on reactions.
9. **Final polish**: Fine-tune individual cuts, add transitions, smooth rhythm.
10. **Export check**: Watch final video. Does pacing serve the story?
